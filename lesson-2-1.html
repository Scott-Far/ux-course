Module 2 — Creative and Good UX Design

Open and closed modes

Scott Faranello

Lesson 2.1 — Open and closed modes

Video coming soon

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  • Explain the difference between open and closed modes of thinking
  • Recognize which mode you are operating in at any given time
  • Describe how the two modes work together
  • Begin applying open mode thinking to your UX practice

In Module 1 we talked about the UX mindset and what it takes to see problems clearly before trying to solve them. Ford immersed himself in real conditions. MacCready questioned the problem everyone else was trying to solve. Both required a particular way of thinking — one that most of us do not spend nearly enough time in.

In this lesson we are going to look at that way of thinking more closely. Where it comes from, what it feels like, and how to get there when you need it most.

The essential mindset for creativity
"Telling people how to be creative is easy; it's only being it that's difficult." — John Cleese

In 1991, comedian and actor John Cleese presented a talk on creativity, providing insight into the creative process along with a recipe for achieving it.

Cleese explained that creativity is not a skill, but rather a "way of operating", and a way of allowing ourselves to be aware of and awake to opportunities in any setting and at any moment. Like the UX mindset, creativity is also a way of operating that allows ideas and solutions to come in ways we might have otherwise missed or misunderstood.

This can often happen in a day-to-day work setting where we are more focused on not messing up than on being messy, which real creativity often requires. Cleese described creativity, and lack thereof, as a pull between two modes of thought, closed and open, both of which are vital for good design.

Closed mode

Closed mode is when we are thinking about tasks, work still to be done, project deadlines, and the need to get our work completed as quickly as possible. Closed mode is not for creativity. Closed mode is for accomplishing specific goals and we have to make decisions and achieve specific objectives. It is the mode for getting things done.

"In closed mode we have inside us a feeling that there's lots to be done and we have to get on with it if we're going to get through it all. It's a mode in which we're probably a little impatient. It's purposeful but not creative." — John Cleese
Open mode

Open mode, by contrast, is much more relaxed, expansive, and less purposeful. In open mode, we are not thinking about deadlines or decisions. We are open to anything. We are thinking more than doing. We are not looking for solutions. We simply allow ourselves to receive.

"In open mode we are more contemplative, more inclined to humor, which always accompanies a wider perspective, and consequently more playful. It is a mood in which curiosity for its own sake can operate because we are not under pressure to get a specific thing done quickly. We can play, and that is what allows our natural creativity to surface." — John Cleese

Closed mode

Purposeful. Deadline-driven. Focused on getting things done. Necessary for execution but not for discovery.

Open mode

Relaxed. Expansive. Curious. Necessary for ideas to form and solutions to emerge unexpectedly.

Open and closed modes in action

To give you an example of open and closed modes in action, here is a short story that explains it quite nicely. The year was 1943, and a naval mechanical engineer named Richard James was developing a tension spring that would be used to keep sensitive shipboard equipment steady, upright, and balanced during rough seas. One day, James accidentally knocked one of the tension springs to the floor. Upon falling, a curious thing happened. The falling spring "stepped in a series of arcs to a stack of books, to a tabletop, and to the floor, where it re-coiled itself and stood upright."

James' discovery became the Slinky, one of the most successful toys in history, but it could have gone a very different way.

In closed mode, dropping a tension spring is a mild annoyance. It bounces. It is hard to locate. Time is wasted. In open mode, that same spring bounces and an idea formulates. Now that spring is no longer a tiny piece of a larger whole. Now, it is a toy.

Cleese shared a similar story about the invention of penicillin.

The inventor, Alexander Fleming, had arranged several dishes for culture to grow upon them. On the day in question, he noticed that one of the dishes had no culture. In closed mode, he would focus on the dishes with the cultures and ignore the one without as perhaps an error on his part and throw it away. "Thank goodness, he was in the open mode, so he became curious about why the culture had not grown on this particular dish. That curiosity, as the world knows, led him to penicillin."

In the closed mode, an idea or an opportunity can be easily missed because it is irrelevant to what we expected to see. In the open mode, it is a clue.

The open mode is where UX practitioners want to spend most of their time. Open mode is where ideas are formed. It allows us to see the world in a different way. It is arguably where people like Henry Ford, Steve Jobs, Paul MacCready, and Bucky Fuller lived a lot of the time in order to see solutions that nobody else could see. To put it another way, closed mode gives us faster horses thinking while open mode gives us the assembly line, the Slinky, and penicillin.

It is hard to make a compelling argument that creativity comes from any other place except our willingness to be open to all ideas and solutions without fear and judgment. In open mode nothing is out of bounds, and anything is possible. In closed mode, we are brought back down to earth.

Using open and closed modes together

If open mode is the creative mode and closed mode helps us to stay focused on completing what we started, then how can we use both to deliver our best work?

"We need to be in the open mode when we are pondering a problem, but once we come up with a solution, we must then switch to the closed mode to implement it. Because once we have made a decision, we are efficient only if we go through with it decisively, undistracted by doubts about its correctness. Once we have taken a decision, we should narrow our focus while we are implementing it, and then after it has been carried out we should once again switch back to the open mode to review the feedback rising from our action, in order to decide whether the course that we have taken is successful, or whether we should continue with the next stage of our plan. And then back into closed mode to implement that next stage, and so on." — John Cleese

We all do this, but most are unaware they are doing it. To become aware allows us the freedom to engage our creative mode on command and carve out time for it, otherwise, we can become stuck in one for too long to the detriment of the other.

And now that you are aware and know the difference, you can be in control when the time comes for each. Make time for both.

Summary

Creativity is not a skill. It is a way of operating. Open mode is where ideas form, where solutions appear unexpectedly, and where the UX mindset lives. Closed mode is where decisions get made and work gets done. Neither is better than the other. Both are necessary. The difference between good work and great work often comes down to knowing which mode you are in and whether it is the right one for the moment.

In the next lesson we will look at the conditions that make open mode possible — what you need to create around yourself to get there when you need it most.
