In the last three lessons we covered color, typography, and layout — the communication tools every designer uses. In this lesson we put them to work in the form everyone in this industry actually uses to think through a design before it is built: the wireframe.

What is a wireframe?

A wireframe is a simplified representation of a screen that shows what content exists and how it is organized, without the distraction of final colors, images, or polish. Think of it as the skeleton of a design. It shows the bones — structure, hierarchy, and flow — before any of the muscle and skin gets added.

Wireframes exist because designing structure and designing appearance are two different problems, and solving them at the same time makes both harder. When you wireframe first, you are forced to answer the real questions before you get distracted by how something looks:

  • What goes where?
  • What is most important on this screen?
  • What does the user need to see first, and what can wait?

If the structure does not work in a wireframe, no amount of color or polish will fix it later. This is the same idea we covered in Lesson 4.1 about starting in black and white, applied to an entire screen rather than just a color decision.

Why lo-fi and mid-fi, not hi-fi

You will see plenty of UX programs that rush students straight into high-fidelity, polished, final-looking designs. This is a mistake, and it is worth understanding why.

Becoming a skilled visual designer who can make a screen look genuinely professional, with refined color, custom imagery, and polished detail takes deliberate practice. This can take years, depending on how proficient you want to be. It is a real craft with its own learning curve, the same way photography or illustration is a craft.

You cannot shortcut that timeline in a few weeks, and trying to will produce work that looks amateurish, which can seriously undermine your confidence at exactly the moment you are trying to build it.

Lo-fi and mid-fi wireframes sidestep this problem entirely. They let you practice exactly what matters most at this stage of your development: hierarchy, typography as communication, color used with intention, and layout that guides the eye. None of that requires visual design mastery. It requires clear thinking, which is what this entire course has been building toward.

This is also a more honest reflection of how UX actually works in practice. Wireframes are a thinking tool. They are meant to be rough. Polish comes later, often from a different person on the team entirely. Learning to wireframe well is learning to think clearly about structure, and is a skill that will serve you for your entire career regardless of how your visual design abilities develop.

Lo-fi and mid-fi compared

Lo-fi wireframe

Basic shapes, lines, and placeholder text only. Square boxes represent images. Lines represent text blocks. Buttons are simple rectangles with labels. Black, white, and grey only. No real content. Fast to make and easy to change. The goal is speed and clarity of structure.

Mid-fi wireframe

Real or near-real content. Basic typography hierarchy. A simple color system — primary, accent, and neutrals. No final imagery or polish. This is where you apply color, type, and layout as communication tools. The screen feels like a real product in progress. This is your target for the Module 4 Project.

What belongs in a wireframe

As you build your wireframe, focus on these decisions:

Structure

What sections exist on this screen and in what order? This is your information architecture from Module 3 made visible.

Hierarchy

What is the most important thing on this screen? Is it the largest, boldest, or most prominent element? If everything looks equally important, nothing is.

Typography

Are you using consistent type sizes for the same kind of content across the screen? Is your hierarchy clear using size and weight alone, even before color is added?

Color with intention

If you introduce color in a mid-fi wireframe, is it doing one of the jobs from Lesson 4.1 — signaling state, creating hierarchy, reinforcing brand, or communicating meaning? If it is doing anything more than this, leave it out.

Layout and spacing

Are elements aligned consistently? Is spacing used to show which things belong together and which things are separate?

What does not belong yet
Good design is just as much about what to show as it is about what not to show.
Imagery

Use placeholder boxes, not real photos or illustrations. Now is not the time to look for those things. Great looking images are not necessary at this stage and will take up too much time better spent elsewhere.

Custom illustration or iconography

Use simple, generic icons if needed. Do not spend time designing custom visuals.

Brand polish

Save logos, custom fonts, and any refined visual styling for later. In a real project setting, there may be someone to do all of that for you. If not, return to it after your wireframe has been reviewed and approved.

Perfectionism

A wireframe that took ten hours to make beautiful misses the point. Speed and clarity of your ideas matters more than visual polish at this stage.

Getting started in Figma

Already know Figma?

Skip ahead to the project brief and get started.

For the Module 4 Project you will build your wireframe in Figma, the industry standard tool for this kind of work.

If you are new to Figma, that is okay because it is pretty intuitive to use and this project will use some of the more basic tools. For this project you will use frames, basic shapes, text, and simple components. You do not need to learn everything Figma can do. That takes time and is best saved for when you actually need to learn it. For now, you need to learn enough to build the wireframe this project requires.

Summary

A wireframe is where thinking becomes visible. Lo-fi gets your structure right quickly. Mid-fi lets you make typography, color, and layout decisions while staying focused on communicating your ideas rather than polishing them. Hi-fidelity visual design is a craft you can pursue later.

The goal of this module was not to make you a visual designer. It was to teach you how to organize and communicate design ideas clearly, simply, and with just the right amount of fidelity so that the viewer can easily understand. Any more than that is a distraction from what a wireframe is supposed to be.

You now have everything you need to make your first wireframe. Have fun!
