In the last three lessons we looked at how information is organized, structured, navigated, and visually presented. In this lesson we look at patterns, which are closely related to IA. Where IA determines how information is organized and presented, patterns help to instantly recognize what the information means and how to interact with it.

What are patterns?

Patterns are everywhere. We use them every day without being aware because when we see them we instantly recognize and understand how to interact with them.

A doorknob is a pattern. A chair is a pattern. An outlet is a pattern. Supermarket aisles are a pattern. Web and mobile design uses patterns too. A search bar, a hamburger menu, form fields, a shopping cart icon, a power button. The list is endless.

Architect Christopher Alexander described patterns as visual cues we recognize because patterns communicate with us "through the connection we feel when we are in their presence."

The most important thing to understand about patterns is that they work best when they are easily recognizable, and where the observer does not have to learn something new in order to understand what it means.

Patterns and mental models

Patterns are an extension of mental models, which we covered in Lesson 3.2. A mental model is the picture a person carries in their mind of how something should look and work based on past experience with similar patterns.

Christopher Alexander identified 15 types of patterns we are familiar with. Here is a simplified version.

The 15 properties (simplified)

As you read through them, think about products and apps you use every day and see how many you can spot.

ScaleImportant things look important. Bigger, bolder, or more prominent elements signal priority.
FocusEvery design has one main thing it wants you to notice first. Everything else supports it.
BoundariesThings that belong together look like they belong together. Things that are separate look separate.
RepetitionThe same patterns appear over and over so you only have to learn them once.
SpaceEmpty space is not wasted space. It gives your eye somewhere to rest and makes content easier to read.
ShapeThings look like what they do. A button looks like something you press. A link looks like something you click.
BalanceThe design feels organized. Nothing feels out of place.
ContrastDifferences in size, color, and weight make it clear what is most important and what is secondary.
ConsistencyThe same types of elements always look and behave the same way. No surprises.
LayersInformation is revealed gradually. You are not hit with everything at once.
SimplicityNothing unnecessary is included. Every element earns its place.
FlowMoving from one screen or step to the next feels smooth and natural.
CalmThe overall experience feels focused, not cluttered.
CharacterThe design feels intentional, not thrown together.
BelongingThe design feels like it fits naturally into the context where it is used.

To go deeper into patterns and read how Alexander described them in terms of building architecture, visit tkwa.com/fifteen-properties or search for Christopher Alexander's A Pattern Language.

When to use patterns

The goal of patterns in your designs is clarity and efficiency. If users of your design need to navigate, search, filter, submit, confirm, or complete a task of any kind, patterns make it easy.

Use patterns that are familiar. Never be clever, because introducing something new is not creative. It is a distraction that makes a design harder than it needs to be.

A pattern that users recognize instantly is always better than one they have to figure out.

If you apply these properties to your designs you will have created something that has what Alexander called the quality that cannot be named — the feeling of being alive, present, and connected.

Pattern libraries and style guides
Pattern libraries

As you design, you will build up a collection of the patterns that you can put into a pattern library. A pattern library is a set of reusable design solutions — buttons, forms, navigation components, cards, input fields, colors, typography, spacing, and so on — that defines how an interface looks, works, and feels.

Use pattern libraries when designing and wireframing. They ensure consistency and they save time by giving you proven patterns for your designs rather than inventing patterns from scratch.

Style guides

Style guides are for documenting all of your design decisions when a design is complete, to be shared with developers and others to ensure that what gets built matches what was designed. Style guides are like blueprints, containing detailed descriptions of colors, sizing, fonts, spacing, and any number of things that show someone building your design exactly what it should look and act like.

Pattern libraries and style guides are living documents, meaning as your designs evolve, both should be updated to reflect those changes.

Summary

Patterns are proven solutions to recurring design problems. They work because they match how users already think and behave when engaging with them. Using familiar patterns is not a lack of creativity. It is the very definition of good design.

The best designers know which patterns to use and which ones not to. That judgment comes from experience, but it starts with learning to see the patterns that are all around you.
