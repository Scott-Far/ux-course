  • Understand the rules of readable type

Typography is not about choosing a font you like. It is about using type as a communication tool. Before a user reads a single word on your screen, the way your type is set tells them what is important, what is secondary, and what to do next. Done well, typography is invisible. Like color, the best typographic decisions are the ones nobody notices.

What type communicates

Like color, typography creates patterns and communicates on two levels simultaneously. The first is content — the words themselves and what they mean. The second is tone — the feeling the type creates before the words are read.

A large, bold headline communicates importance or visual separation. A small, light caption communicates supporting information. A button label communicates action. Italics can mean a quote or something additional. Using all capital letters communicates attention or volume.

Typography is one of the most powerful tools in a UI designer's kit. Every typographic decision creates a pattern and a signal. The key is making sure your signals are intentional.

Hierarchy through type

Visual hierarchy is created in a number of ways using type — through size, weight, position, space, contrast, and alignment. When applied, these properties tell users how to read a page before they read it.

If these principles feel familiar, they should. Size, weight, position, space, contrast, and alignment are all expressions of the patterns we covered in Module 3 — Scale, Focus, Contrast, Space, and Balance are all at work here, just applied to type.
SizeSize communicates priority. The largest type on a page is the most important. Page titles are larger than section headings, which are larger than body text, which is larger than captions. If everything is the same size, nothing is a priority.
WeightBold type draws the eye before regular type. It creates weight that signals to the reader the most important words within a body of text.
PositionPosition establishes reading order. Type at the top of a page or section is read first. Type on the left edge is read before type on the right. Design your type in the order in which you want people to read it. The eye needs a place to go when engaging with design and it is the designer's job to direct it.
SpaceSpace is a natural visual separator between thoughts and ideas. It is directional and creates a sense of balance and maneuverability through design.
ContrastDark colors should only ever be used on a light background, and vice versa. Text needs contrast against the background so that it is effortless to read. If a user has to squint or the text cannot be easily read then the contrast is not enough.
AlignmentLeft-aligned text is the easiest to read in most languages. Centered text is harder to read in long blocks because the eye has to find a new starting point on every line. Right-aligned text is even harder. Use center and right alignment sparingly and intentionally, never as a default.
Choosing fonts

You do not need to be a typography expert to choose the right font. You just need to understand a few simple principles and make decisions that serve the design rather than your personal taste.

Stick to one font. Two fonts maximum. There should never be a need to use more than one font in any design. This excludes logos as those may have specific branding requirements. But for content, one font coupled with the hierarchy principles we just discussed is all you will ever need.

Use sans-serif for screens. When designing for screens, always use fonts without serifs, which are the small decorative strokes at the ends of letterforms.

System sans-serif — clean and screen-ready
Sans-serif fonts are generally more readable on screens, especially at small sizes. Helvetica, Arial, Inter, and Roboto are all good examples. These are also default fonts built into operating systems and are designed specifically for screens. Using them is not lazy or boring. It is the most practical choice. Serif fonts like Georgia or Times New Roman work well for long-form reading and are generally used for published books. There is good reason for this — serifs help the eye follow lines of text on paper. On screens, especially at small sizes, sans-serif is the safer and more practical choice.

Never use decorative fonts in UI. Script fonts, handwritten fonts, and decorative display fonts have no place in UI design. They are hard to read, they undermine clarity, and they signal that the designer prioritized style over communication.

Consistency in font choice is an expression of the Repetition and Consistency properties from Module 3. When users encounter the same font treatment across a design they learn the system once and apply it everywhere. Changing fonts or type styles without reason breaks that system and creates uncertainty.
What poor typography signals

Poor typography signals the same things poor color use does — that the designer prioritized appearance over communication and does not understand how visual design works as a system.

When the properties of good design are ignored in typography, users feel it before they can name it — the same way they feel when IA is broken or when color is used without intention. The discomfort is real even when the cause is invisible.

Specific things that quickly undermine confidence in your work:

  • Too many fonts looks chaotic and unplanned
  • Inconsistent sizing — if headings are different sizes on different pages for no reason, users feel the design is unreliable
  • Text that is too small to read comfortably signals that the designer did not consider the user's experience
  • All caps for body text is significantly harder to read and can feel like the text is shouting
  • Decorative fonts in functional contexts signals that the designer does not understand the difference between branding and UI
  • Poor alignment signals that the designer does not understand balance and symmetry
Summary

Typography is communication. Every decision a designer makes about size, weight, spacing, and font choice tells users something before they read a word. Use hierarchy to guide the eye. Use restraint to avoid noise. Use readability rules to ensure that what you designed can actually be used.

Like color, the best typographic decisions are the ones nobody notices. The goal is not beautiful type. The goal is type that disappears into the experience and lets the content speak for itself.
