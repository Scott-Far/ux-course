In the last two lessons we covered what IA is, where it comes from, and the tools practitioners use to build it. In this lesson we look at how it all works together in the real world.

The best way to understand good IA is to see it in action in products you already use. Once you know what to look for, you will start seeing it everywhere.

Amazon

For over two decades, Amazon has led the way in designing a highly usable experience that demonstrates every principle we have covered in this module. Consider what Amazon does when you land on their homepage. Without any instruction, you immediately know what the site is for, where to search, how to navigate, and what to do next.

Nothing requires explanation. The design does the work. This is not accidental.

Amazon's IA is built around a deeply understood mental model of online shopping and how people search. Every decision in their information architecture serves that model. The search bar is prominent because finding things is the primary task. Categories are organized around how customers think about products, not how Amazon organizes its warehouse. Related items, purchase history, and recommendations all reflect what a specific user has already shown interest in.

Amazon also demonstrates all Four Cs:

Coordination

Thousands of products, categories, and promotions presented simultaneously without overwhelming the user. Every element points toward the same destination.

Cooperation

Distractions are everywhere on Amazon — ads, recommendations, deals, similar items, reviews — but none of them prevent you from completing your original task. If anything, they enhance the experience and encourage your purchase more than stopping it.

Change

Amazon is highly scalable and built for change and expansion. The company is too big and too familiar to suddenly change everything, but they have designed it to continuously expand without disrupting the underlying experience.

Consequence

The outcome of Amazon's IA is measurable at every level. Users find what they need. Purchases are completed. People come back. And the business is one of the most successful retail operations in history.

What good IA feels like

When IA is working, you do not notice it. You arrive at a destination. You find what you need. You complete your task. The experience feels effortless because the structure is doing its job invisibly.

When IA is failing, you feel it immediately. You cannot find what you are looking for. You retrace your steps. You wonder whether what you need even exists. You give up and call customer support or go somewhere else.

The most important thing to understand about IA as a designer is this: your job is to create a navigation so obvious and so aligned with how your users think that they never have to think about it at all.

Knowing how people think when interacting with design is the most important job for a designer The good news is you do not need a PhD in psychology to do this well. You just have to know patterns. Luckily, you will learn all about patterns in the next module.
Questions to gauge your IA

As you design and evaluate IA, ask yourself these questions and make sure you can answer them.

  • Is the information coordinated in a way that is quickly and easily understandable?
  • How much effort is required to engage with your design?
  • How do you know? Have you tested it with real users?
  • Is the right amount of information presented? Too much? Too little? Just right?
  • Can your design change and grow without disrupting the existing foundation?
  • What are the consequences if users cannot find what they need? Not just for customers, but for the company?

These questions are your checklist. With experience they will eventually become second nature, but until then keep referring back to this list until they do.

Summary

Good IA is invisible when it works and immediately noticeable when it does not. Amazon succeeds because its structure is built around a deeply understood mental model of online shopping and was built for change without disruption.

Mental models are your frame. Taxonomy and visual hierarchy are your tools. Sitemaps are your blueprint. Use them together and you have everything you need. The rest is just practice.
